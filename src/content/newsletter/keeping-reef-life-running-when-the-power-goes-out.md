## Before we get to batteries: What's actually keeping your reef alive?

When the lights go out and the house goes quiet, it’s easy to panic and think you need a massive industrial generator to keep every single piece of gear running. You don't. One of the biggest mistakes I see people make is trying to power their entire display—lights, heaters, and decorative features—during a blackout. That isn't how this works. 

Your reef life depends on three primary things: oxygenation, water movement, and protein skimming (if you use one). Most hobbyists can survive an hour or two without the lights being on; the fish won't mind sitting in the dark for a bit. What they can't handle is stagnant water. If your return pump stops and your skimmer dies, oxygen levels drop and waste starts to build up quickly. Your goal when planning a backup system is strictly "life support." You want enough power to keep the water moving and the protein skimmer spinning. Everything else—the fancy LED strips, the heaters (unless it’s a deep freeze), and any secondary pumps—can stay off until the grid comes back online.

## Figuring out what you need: The load calculation part.

![Hands-on technique: A person using a water testing kit to take a sample reading from the overflow weir in an aquarium sump.](/images/newsletter/keeping-reef-life-running-when-the-power-goes-out-section1.png)


To figure out how much battery capacity you actually need, you have to stop guessing and start looking at your equipment labels. Every pump or skimmer has a wattage rating on it. You’ll want to add up the watts of only the "must-have" items we talked about above. 

Once you have that total number, you have to decide how long you want to stay powered. Let's say your essential gear pulls 200 watts in total. If you want to keep them running for four hours during a storm, you need to know the "Watt-hours." You multiply those two numbers together (200 watts x 4 hours = 800 Watt-hours). This is your baseline. Keep in mind that batteries aren't perfect; they lose some energy as heat and through the inverter process, so you generally want a backup capacity that is about 20% higher than your calculated need just to have a safety buffer.

## The power station thing: What does '1 kWh' actually mean for my tank?

I see these big portable power stations at the hardware store with "1 kWh" or "2000Wh" printed on the side in huge letters, and it can be confusing to translate that into what your fish actually need. Think of 1 kWh (kilowatt-hour) as 1,000 Watt-hours. If your essential reef equipment pulls about 300 watts total—which is pretty common for a mid-sized setup with a decent skimmer and return pump—a 1 kWh station would theoretically give you about three hours of runtime.

It’s important to remember that these numbers are "usable" capacity, but the inverter (the part that turns battery power into wall-plug power) also uses energy just to stay turned on. In my experience, if your tank's life support pulls 250 watts, a 1 kWh unit might give you closer to two and a half hours of actual runtime rather than four. It’s always better to over-estimate your needs slightly so you aren't watching the battery percentage drop to zero while you're still waiting for the power to come back on.

## What trips people up when sizing the setup.

![A close-up of various electrical connections and hoses laid out neatly on a workbench next to power supplies, suggesting planning or troubleshooting equipment.](/images/newsletter/keeping-reef-life-running-when-the-power-goes-out-section2.png)


The most common mistake I see is "the hidden load." People look at their main return pump and think that’s all they need to power, but they forget about the small stuff. Does your skimmer have a separate internal pump? Do you have a secondary flow pump in a rock pile for oxygenation? If those stay off while the main pump stays on, you might end up with "dead zones" in the tank where water isn't moving, which can lead to localized issues.

Another common pitfall is assuming all electronics draw the same amount of power. A high-end protein skimmer can pull significantly more juice than a small wave maker. If you don't account for that heavy hitter, your battery will drain much faster than you planned. Finally, people often forget about "startup surge." Some pumps require a bit more energy to get moving from a dead stop. While most modern DC pumps are pretty efficient, it’s always safer to assume your gear might pull slightly more than the "running" wattage listed on the sticker during those first few seconds of operation.

## A simple plan for peace of mind (The Checklist)

Don't wait for a summer thunderstorm to find out if your backup system actually works. You need to test it when things are calm so you know exactly how long that battery lasts under your specific load. Here is a practical way to get organized:

*   **Audit your gear:** Write down the wattage of every pump and skimmer in your display.
*   **Define "Critical":** Circle only the items that keep the water moving and oxygenated.
*   **Calculate runtime:** Multiply those watts by 4 hours (or your preferred duration) to find your target Watt-hour capacity.
*   **The Dry Run:** Plug your critical gear into your backup power station while the main house power is off. Time how long it lasts. 
*   **Update your notes:** If it lasted less time than you expected, you either need a bigger battery or you need to turn off one of the non-essential pumps during an outage.

Having a tested plan takes the stress out of the situation. When the lights go out, you won't be scrambling; you'll just flip the switch and know your reef is safe.
